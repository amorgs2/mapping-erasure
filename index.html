As of September 2026: every collection in this study that can be searched in full from a distance is held in the United States or in France, the two countries that dug the canal. Even the Panamanian and Caribbean newspapers here can be read only through the Library of Congress and the University of Florida. The Museo del Canal in Panama City serves catalog metadata only, and the registers of the West Indian workers known as the Silver Men have not been digitized anywhere. That asymmetry is itself part of the record of erasure.

How to read this map

Using the controls:
1
Choose a view. Each one colours the same places by a different question. Start is the opening state: choose it again to clear everything and begin afresh.
2
Move the time slider, or press Play, to run from 1850 to 2025. Play slows through the construction decade, where most of the change happens.
3
Show all puts every placement back on screen at once, in whichever view you are in, ignoring the year.
4
Click any place, on the map or in a panel, to open its record card; it turns red on the map. Drag the card by its title bar; close it with the ×, Escape, or a double-click outside.
5
Type in the find box to search both place panels by any name the sources record, so a renamed place still turns up under its earlier name.
6
The showing switch chooses places or the buildings & infrastructure inside them. The filter menus narrow that down further.
7
sources disagree keeps only contested locations. american origin fills the townsites the United States canal authority built for its Gold Roll (mostly white American) and Silver Roll (mostly West Indian Black) workers.
8
In the Sources panel, click a source to redraw the map with only the places it records. Sources and Located places can be sorted by date or A–Z.
9
The dot beside each name carries the colour the map is giving that place right now; unlocated places keep a neutral grey ring. From 2000 a closing tally joins the caption.
10
Each ? explains the reasoning behind a design choice. The page address records the current display, so it can be copied to cite one particular view.
The map symbols:

Shape shows how confidently a place is located; colour depends on the current view; opacity shows the state of the evidence for the selected year; places that are absent from the record are still shown as such. Fixed-size symbols do not represent distance on the ground.

1A precisely located feature, drawn as a solid point.
2The same symbol, faint: the place is claimed to exist in this year, but no dated source covers it.
3An extent drawn at the radius its source states, translucent so that neighbouring extents can overlap.
4A striped area: the place lay somewhere within it, but its position is only loosely known.
5A dashed symbol of fixed size: located, but with no extent on record.
6Hollow rings joined by a line: sources place the same feature differently, so every claim is plotted and none is chosen.
7A dotted ring: removed by the year on the slider, but still shown as a trace. Dotted outlines are only used for removed places.
8A dashed halo: an unlocated place that the sources tie to the located place it surrounds.
9Solid red, with the american origin switch on: a townsite the United States canal authority built for its Gold Roll (mostly white American) or Silver Roll (mostly West Indian Black) workers, in any view. A red outline alone marks whichever place's record card is currently open.
10A traced outline: the shape the place actually had, digitised from a historical map or town plan rather than approximated by a circle. Most of the buildings and infrastructure are drawn this way, and a town keeps its traced extent underneath them.
